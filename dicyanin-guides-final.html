Critical Safety & Legal Disclaimer

This page describes hazardous laboratory chemistry and the historical construction of optical filters. Dicyanin (dicyanine) is a toxic synthetic dye. The reagents required for its synthesis (sodium metal, ethyl iodide, sodium sulfide, chloroform, strong bases, organic solvents) are flammable, reactive, toxic, or regulated. Attempting synthesis outside a properly equipped laboratory with a fume hood, PPE, waste disposal, and experienced supervision is extremely dangerous and may be illegal depending on local regulations.

The “aura-viewing” claims associated with Kilner screens have never been independently scientifically validated. Reported visual effects are consistent with ordinary colored-filter afterimages, contrast enhancement, and expectation. Prolonged viewing was noted by Kilner himself to cause eye pain.

Do not attempt any of these procedures at home or without professional laboratory facilities and training. This document is provided strictly for educational and historical reference.

1 Synthesis of Dicyanin (Dicyanine A)

Dicyanin is a synthetic carbocyanine dye historically used as a photographic sensitizer for the red and near-infrared region. Chemically it is built from quinoline ring systems linked by a polymethine bridge (typical formula range C25H25IN2 for the iodide salt).

The improved laboratory method below is based primarily on Palkin’s 1923 work, which gave substantially higher yields than the earlier Mikeska–Haller–Adams sodium-ethylate route.

Materials Required (Laboratory Scale)

  • 2,4-Dimethyl-6-ethoxyquinoline (starting base)
  • Ethyl iodide (iodoethane)
  • Sodium sulfide nonahydrate (Na2S·9H2O)
  • 95% ethanol and absolute ethanol
  • Chloroform (catalyst)
  • Diethyl ether (for washing)
  • Sodium metal (only for older sodium-ethylate method)
  • Standard organic lab glassware, steam/controlled bath (~50°C), suction filtration
  • Full PPE: chemical-resistant gloves, goggles, lab coat, fume hood
1

Prepare the Intermediate (Ethiodide)

Combine 2,4-dimethyl-6-ethoxyquinoline with roughly an equal weight of ethyl iodide. Heat briefly on a steam bath until solid. Wash with diethyl ether. Recrystallize several times from boiling 95% ethanol. Dry. This quaternary salt is the key intermediate.

2

Prepare Alcoholic Sodium Sulfide Solution

Boil ~25 g Na2S·9H2O in 100 mL of 95% ethanol for ~2 minutes. Cool and filter. This is the working alkaline solution.

3

Condensation to Form the Dye (Palkin Method)

Use ~1 g ethiodide intermediate per 25 mL alcoholic sodium sulfide solution. Dissolve intermediate in the (warm) sulfide solution. Quickly add ~1.5 mL chloroform per gram intermediate as catalyst. Warm at ~50°C for 30 minutes (up to ~1 hour). Cool. The dye precipitates.

4

Isolation and Washing

Filter by suction. Wash successively with 95% ethanol, diethyl ether, water (to remove salts), then ethanol and ether again. Air-dry, protected from light. Product is typically a dark green crystalline solid with metallic sheen. Solutions appear intensely blue/green and may show dichroism.

Earlier routes used KOH + atmospheric oxygen on α,γ-dimethylquinolinium salts, or sodium ethylate/methylate in absolute alcohol. Yields were very low and products often contaminated with cyanines and tar. Palkin’s sodium-sulfide + chloroform procedure improved recovery by roughly an order of magnitude.

Historical yields remained modest. Spectroscopic checks (absorption near ~670 nm vs ~620 nm for accompanying cyanine) and photographic sensitization tests were used to evaluate product quality.

2 Kilner-Style Screens / Glasses

Walter J. Kilner (medical electrician at St. Thomas’ Hospital, London) described glass cells or screens containing alcoholic solutions of dicyanin in his 1911 book The Human Atmosphere (later The Human Aura). He claimed viewing through these screens trained the eyes to perceive a faint envelope around the body.

Independent scientific verification of any paranormal effect has never been obtained; visual phenomena are consistent with ordinary filter after-effects.

Materials Required

  • Dicyanin (or dicyanine A) powder — toxic, scarce, degrades with light/heat
  • Absolute or high-purity ethanol
  • Two clean, flat optical glass plates or high-quality microscope slides
  • Thin spacers (~0.1–0.5 mm)
  • Sealant compatible with alcohol (optical epoxy, paraffin wax, or gasket)
  • Pipette/dropper, clean glass vial
  • Dark, cool storage
  • Protective equipment (gloves, eye protection, ventilation)
1

Prepare Stock Dye Solution

Dissolve a small quantity of dicyanin powder in ethanol. Historical starting point: ~0.1 g dye in 100 mL alcohol (~1:1000). Stir gently. Solution is typically deep blue-green to violet. Keep cool and fully protected from light. Use fresh; solution does not keep well.

2

Clean and Prepare Glass Plates

Thoroughly clean both plates of dust, grease, and fingerprints. Dry completely. Residue creates optical defects or degradation sites.

3

Assemble the Liquid Cell

Place thin spacers along edges of one plate (gap typically 0.1–0.5 mm). Pipette dye solution to fill the gap when the second plate is applied. Lower the second plate carefully, excluding air bubbles. Thin path length keeps the screen usable while providing strong coloration.

4

Seal the Edges

Seal all edges thoroughly so alcohol cannot evaporate and liquid cannot leak. Optical epoxy or wax was used historically. Result is a flat glass sandwich. Kilner used both darker screens (brief initial sky viewing) and lighter ones for observation.

Historical procedure: Controlled lighting (often north-facing window + dark background). Look through a dark screen at bright sky 30–45 seconds, then switch to a lighter screen (or none after “training”) while viewing a subject against a dark background. Kilner noted prolonged use caused painful eyes.

Modern considerations: True dicyanin is hard to obtain, expensive, toxic, and unstable. Modern “dicyanin-style” filters often use stable coatings or alternative dyes approximating the transmission curve. Safer substitutes (cobalt-blue/purple glass, certain theatrical gels) exist but are not chemically identical. Liquid cells still risk leakage and degradation.
