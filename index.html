Overview

What is dicyanin?

Dicyanin (dicyanine / dicyanine A) is a synthetic carbocyanine dye. Two nitrogen-containing ring systems are linked by a short conjugated carbon chain (a polymethine bridge). A common form is the iodide salt, roughly C25H25IN2.

It was developed in the early 20th century as a photographic sensitizer. Ordinary silver-halide plates mostly responded to blue light; dyes like dicyanin pushed useful sensitivity into the red and toward the near-infrared.

Appearance

Dark green crystals with metallic sheen. Solutions: intense blue-green to violet.

Original use

Extending photographic plate response toward deep red / near-IR.

Kilner link

Alcoholic solutions sealed between glass plates for 1911 “aura screen” experiments.

Today

Obscure, toxic, unstable — not a normal commercial product.

Critical

Safety

Not a home project

Dicyanin is toxic. Historical synthesis uses ethyl iodide, sodium sulfide, chloroform, and strong bases — flammable, reactive, or regulated chemicals.

Work only in a proper laboratory with a fume hood, training, and full PPE. This site is historical documentation, not permission to experiment casually.

Kilner’s aura claims were never independently scientifically validated.

Hazards called out by the old chemistry

  • Toxic dye dust and residues
  • Flammable solvents (ethanol, ether)
  • Chloroform vapor
  • Sulfide solutions (odor + reactivity)
  • Eye strain from prolonged viewing through dense filters
Chemistry

Properties

Dicyanin belongs to the cyanine / carbocyanine family. The conjugated bridge between the rings creates strong visible absorption — the reason these dyes were useful as sensitizers.

  • Solid: dark green, often with metallic luster
  • Solution: deep blue-green to violet; can show dichroism (color changing with depth)
  • Absorption: deep red region (historically discussed near ~670 nm in some preparations)
  • Stability: limited — light, heat, and time degrade solutions
  • Classic screen solvent: ethanol

Related names in the same literature: cyanines, isocyanines, other carbocyanines. Dicyanin was one specialized member of that toolkit.

Materials

Dye materials

From early laboratory write-ups (especially Palkin 1923). Lab scale only.

Chemicals

  • 2,4-Dimethyl-6-ethoxyquinoline (starting base)
  • Ethyl iodide (iodoethane)
  • Sodium sulfide nonahydrate (Na2S·9H2O)
  • 95% ethanol and absolute ethanol
  • Chloroform (small catalytic amount in Palkin’s method)
  • Diethyl ether (washing)

Equipment

  • Standard organic glassware
  • Controlled heating bath ~50°C / steam bath
  • Suction filtration and recrystallization setup
  • Fume hood, chemical-resistant gloves, goggles, lab coat
Step-by-step

Make the dye

Outline of the Palkin-style laboratory sequence. Earlier sodium-ethylate routes existed but gave worse yields. Historical procedure only — not a green light for home chemistry.

1

Prepare the ethiodide intermediate

Combine 2,4-dimethyl-6-ethoxyquinoline with roughly an equal weight of ethyl iodide.

  • Warm briefly on a steam bath until the mixture solidifies
  • Wash the solid with diethyl ether
  • Recrystallize several times from boiling 95% ethanol
  • Dry — this quaternary salt is the key intermediate
2

Make alcoholic sodium sulfide solution

Boil about 25 g Na2S·9H2O in 100 mL of 95% ethanol for ~2 minutes. Cool and filter.

3

Condense to form the dye

Use about 1 g ethiodide per 25 mL sulfide solution.

  • Dissolve the intermediate in the (warm) sulfide solution
  • Add about 1.5 mL chloroform per gram of intermediate (catalyst)
  • Hold near 50°C for about 30 minutes
  • Cool — the dye precipitates
4

Isolate and wash

  • Filter by suction
  • Wash with 95% ethanol, then diethyl ether, then water
  • Wash again with ethanol and ether
  • Air-dry protected from strong light

Product: dark green crystalline solid with metallic sheen.

Period quality checks sometimes used absorption spectra (dicyanine band vs other cyanines). Yields remained modest even with Palkin’s improvements.

Materials

Screen materials

Pattern from Kilner-style liquid cells (1911 descriptions and later reconstructions).

What goes into a cell

  • Dicyanin (or dicyanine A) — toxic, scarce, light-sensitive
  • High-purity ethanol
  • Two clean flat optical glass plates or quality slides
  • Thin spacers (~0.1–0.5 mm)
  • Alcohol-compatible sealant (historically wax / early cements)
  • Pipette, clean vial, dark cool storage
  • Gloves and eye protection

True dicyanin is hard to source. Many modern “aura glasses” are ordinary colored filters, not sealed dye cells.

Step-by-step

Make the screens

Historical pattern for a Kilner-style liquid cell. Documentation of an old method — not a recommendation to handle toxic dye casually.

1

Make a dilute stock solution

Dissolve a small amount of dicyanin in ethanol — on the order of 0.1 g in 100 mL (~1:1000) in later retellings. Deep blue-green to violet. Keep cool, dark, and reasonably fresh.

2

Clean the glass

Remove all dust, grease, and fingerprints from both plates. Dry completely.

3

Build the liquid gap

  • Place thin spacers along the edges of one plate (gap ~0.1–0.5 mm)
  • Pipette dye solution to fill the gap
  • Lower the second plate carefully; work out air bubbles
4

Seal the edges

Seal all around so ethanol cannot evaporate. Result: a flat glass sandwich with a thin colored liquid layer inside.

5

Historical viewing pattern

  • Controlled light (Kilner preferred a north window and dark background)
  • Brief view of bright sky through a darker screen
  • Then observe a subject through a lighter screen
  • He warned that long sessions could cause eye pain
Timeline

History

Early 1900s

Cyanine sensitizing dyes, including dicyanin-type materials, enter photographic chemistry to push plate response toward the red.

1911

Walter J. Kilner publishes The Human Atmosphere, describing dicyanin screens and aura observations.

1912

Kilner’s note on chromatic focus shifts after using a dicyanin screen.

1920

Mikeska, Haller & Adams publish a synthesis of dicyanine A in the Journal of the American Chemical Society.

1923

Palkin reports improved laboratory conditions and better recovery in Industrial & Engineering Chemistry.

1924+

Structural work clarifies the carbocyanine nature of the dicyanines. Photography moves toward more stable sensitizers.

FAQ

Common questions

Is dicyanin still manufactured?

Not in any ordinary commercial sense. It is obscure, toxic, and unstable.

Did the screens show a real aura?

There is no good scientific evidence for that. Ordinary effects from strong colored filters — afterimages, contrast changes, expectation — explain the reports more simply.

Why did photographers want this dye?

Early plates ignored much of the red end of the spectrum. Sensitizing dyes extended the usable range toward deep red and near-IR.

Are modern “dicyanin glasses” authentic?

Usually not. Most are standard colored filters or marketing approximations, not sealed historical dye cells.

Can this be done safely at home?

Synthesis: no. Casual handling of authentic dye: no. This page documents historical practice only.
