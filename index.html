Overview

What is dicyanin?

Dicyanin is a synthetic carbocyanine dye — two rings linked by a conjugated carbon chain. Typical form: iodide salt (~C25H25IN2).

Built as a photographic sensitizer so plates could respond to red and near-infrared, not only blue. Kilner later used alcoholic solutions in sealed glass cells (1911).

Look

Dark green crystals; deep blue-green solutions.

Then

Red / near-IR plate sensitization.

Kilner

Alcoholic solutions in glass screens, 1911.

Now

Rare, toxic, unstable.

Critical

Safety

Not a home project

Dicyanin is toxic. Old routes use ethyl iodide, sodium sulfide, chloroform, strong bases.

Lab only: fume hood, training, PPE. Historical documentation ≠ permission to experiment casually.

Main hazards

  • Toxic dye dust / residues
  • Flammable solvents (ethanol, ether)
  • Chloroform vapor · sulfide solutions
  • Eye strain from dense filters

Context

Myth vs record

On the record

  • Dicyanin was a real photographic sensitizer
  • Synthesis papers exist (e.g. 1920, 1923)
  • Kilner published screen methods in 1911
  • Strong colored filters change what you see

Not established

  • That screens reveal a biological “aura”
  • Medical diagnosis via Kilner screens
  • That modern “dicyanin glasses” are authentic cells
  • That this chemistry is safe for home use

Afterimages, contrast shifts, and expectation explain most aura reports without new physics.

Materials

Dye materials

Everything below is from historical laboratory recipes (especially Palkin’s 1923 outline and related early papers). Amounts scale; the idea is “small lab batch,” not industrial production.

Chemicals — what they are and why they show up

  • 2,4-Dimethyl-6-ethoxyquinoline — the main organic “base.” This is the ring system that becomes half of the dye structure after the reaction sequence.
  • Ethyl iodide (iodoethane) — used to turn the base into a salt (an ethiodide). That salt is the intermediate that later links into the dye.
  • Sodium sulfide nonahydrate (Na2S·9H2O) — dissolved in alcohol to make the alkaline sulfide solution used in the condensation step of the Palkin-style route.
  • 95% ethanol — workhorse solvent for dissolving, boiling the sulfide, recrystallizing, and washing.
  • Stronger / absolute ethanol — used when the recipe calls for drier alcohol (recrystallization / final washes depending on the write-up).
  • Chloroform — in Palkin’s method, a small “catalytic” amount is added during condensation. Volatile and hazardous; historical lab only.
  • Diethyl ether — cold wash solvent to help remove impurities from the intermediate and the crude dye.

Typical scale cues from the old write-ups

  • Ethiodide formation: base + about an equal weight of ethyl iodide.
  • Sulfide solution: about 25 g sodium sulfide nonahydrate in 100 mL of 95% ethanol.
  • Condensation ratio: about 1 g ethiodide per 25 mL sulfide solution, plus about 1.5 mL chloroform per gram of ethiodide.

Equipment

  • Standard organic glassware (flasks, condenser if needed, beakers, graduated cylinders)
  • Controlled heating: water / steam bath around 50°C for condensation; ability to boil ethanol briefly for the sulfide solution
  • Ice or cold water for cooling / crystallization
  • Suction filtration (Büchner funnel + flask + vacuum source)
  • Clean filter paper; dark storage vials for the finished dye
  • Fume hood, chemical-resistant gloves, goggles, lab coat — non-negotiable in any real lab setting

What “success” looked like historically

  • Intermediate: a cleaned, recrystallized salt (not the final green dye yet).
  • Final dye: dark green crystalline solid, often with a metallic sheen; solutions look deep blue-green to violet.

Reality check

Several of these chemicals are toxic, flammable, or both. This list is for understanding historical sources — not a shopping list for a kitchen experiment.

Guide

Make the dye

This is a maximally detailed historical laboratory walkthrough of dicyanine A–type dye preparation, based mainly on S. Palkin, Ind. Eng. Chem. 15 (1923) 379–381, with ethiodide prep from Mikeska, Haller & Adams, JACS 42 (1920) 2392–2394, and parallel detail from early process patents that used the same chemistry. It is written so a careful reader can understand every stage. It is not permission to run this outside a trained lab.

Step 1 of 10
1

What this synthesis is actually doing

Dicyanine A is a carbocyanine dye: two substituted quinoline rings joined by a conjugated carbon chain. The laboratory route does not start from the finished dye. It starts from a quinoline base, converts that base into a quaternary salt (the ethiodide intermediate), then uses an alkaline alcoholic medium so two of those units link into the deep-colored dye.

Two historical condensation families exist:

  • Mikeska–Haller–Adams (1920): sodium methylate/ethylate in absolute alcohol, often via the ethnitrate, in the presence of air. Yields were very low (on the order of ~0.025 g dye per gram intermediate in Palkin’s comparison).
  • Palkin (1923): sodium sulfide in 95% alcohol, with a small amount of chloroform. Palkin reported yields on the order of twelve times higher than the older average under his improved conditions.

The walkthrough below follows the Palkin-style sulfide + chloroform path because it is the one most later summaries mean by “improved dicyanine synthesis,” and because the paper and related patents spell out concentrations, washes, and temperatures more explicitly.

2

Hazards and lab setup (read before any weighing)

Hazard map:

  • Ethyl iodide — volatile, toxic alkylating agent; vapor exposure is serious.
  • Sodium sulfide solutions — corrosive/alkaline; can release hydrogen sulfide under acid conditions; strong odor.
  • Chloroform — volatile, toxic, regulated in many jurisdictions.
  • Diethyl ether — extremely flammable; peroxide risk in old bottles.
  • Ethanol — flammable; large volumes under heat need good control.
  • Dicyanine product — treated as a toxic dye dust/residue problem.

Required setting: chemical fume hood, eye protection, chemical-resistant gloves, lab coat, fire control, labeled waste streams. No kitchen, no garage “just this once.”

Before starting: verify the hood draws; stage clean glassware; prepare ice/cold water; label vials for (a) ethiodide intermediate and (b) final dye; plan sulfide and halogenated-solvent waste.

3

Prepare 2,4-dimethyl-6-ethoxyquinoline ethiodide (the intermediate)

This is the quaternary salt intermediate. Mikeska, Haller & Adams describe forming it from the free base and ethyl iodide.

Materials concept:

  • 2,4-Dimethyl-6-ethoxyquinoline (the base)
  • Ethyl iodide in about an equal weight relative to the base

Procedure as described in the 1920 lineage:

  • Combine the base with roughly its own weight of ethyl iodide.
  • Warm the mixture. The reaction mass is expected to become solid.
  • After cooling, wash the solid in the flask with diethyl ether to strip residual ethyl iodide and oily impurities.
  • Recrystallize several times from boiling 95% ethanol: dissolve in hot 95% alcohol, cool to crystallize, filter, repeat until the solid looks clean.
  • Dry the cleaned ethiodide before using it in condensation.

Scale note from the 1920 paper: for their ethiodide preparation they indicate a yield on the order of about 10 g under the conditions they quote for that step (see the original for the exact base charge they used).

What you are trying to obtain: a crystalline ethiodide salt, not the green final dye. If the solid is tarry after one crystallization, that is exactly why they recrystallize more than once.

Analytical check they cared about: nitrogen content of the ethiodide was compared with theory for C15H20NOI (they report Kjeldahl values near the calculated ~3.9% N).

4

Make the alcoholic sodium sulfide reagent

Palkin’s improved process depends on sodium sulfide in 95% alcohol, not sodium ethylate in absolute alcohol.

Standard concentration used as the basis of his study:

  • Boil 25 g Na2S·9H2O in 100 mL (or 100 parts) of 95% ethanol for about 2 minutes (related patent wording sometimes says about 3 minutes).
  • Cool to the intended reaction temperature.
  • Filter (or decant) so undissolved solids do not enter the condensation flask.

Why 95% alcohol: Palkin explicitly preferred 95% alcohol over absolute alcohol as the medium for the sulfide process after comparative trials.

Handling: prepare fresh; keep covered in the hood; do not acidify (H2S risk). Measure the clear filtrate by volume for the next step.

5

Charge the condensation: ratios that define the Palkin process

Core ratio used throughout Palkin’s comparative work:

  • About 1 g of ethiodide intermediate per 25 mL of the alcoholic sodium sulfide solution prepared as above.
  • About 1.5 mL chloroform per gram of ethiodide (Palkin’s optimized chloroform proportion).

Operational sequence from the paper’s general method:

  1. Place the measured volume of filtered alcoholic sodium sulfide in an Erlenmeyer flask.
  2. Dissolve the ethiodide in that solution.
  3. Add the measured chloroform quickly.
  4. Warm in a controlled bath for the chosen time/temperature.

Patent-scale worked example (same chemistry family): dissolve 4 parts ethiodide in 100 parts of the hot (~50°C) alcoholic sulfide solution, add 9.5 parts chloroform, keep at 50°C on a water bath for about 2 hours with stirring preferred — or, alternatively, stand at room temperature about 24 hours. The mixture becomes dark blue.

Temperature window in Palkin’s tables: many productive runs cluster around roughly 50–70°C depending on exact trial; uncontrolled hard boiling is not the point — controlled warmth is.

What “working” looks like: deep coloration develops; on cooling, dye solid separates and can be filtered.

6

Time, temperature, and process choices (why yields varied)

Palkin’s paper is largely a study of variables: solvent, alkalinity source, water, oxygen, catalyst (chloroform), time, and temperature. Key takeaways for a reader of the method:

  • Sodium sulfide + chloroform beat sodium ethylate alone by a large factor under his conditions.
  • Chloroform was not a vague “dash”; proportional trials settled near 1.5 mL per gram intermediate with the 25 g/100 mL sulfide stock.
  • Too little time/temperature → incomplete conversion. Too abusive heating → more side products / lower useful dye.
  • He observed related sensitizing dye species spectroscopically; the desired dicyanine absorption he discusses is around the deep-red region historically associated with ~6700 Å (670 nm) class sensitization maxima in that literature.

Practical notebook discipline: record exact grams, mL, bath temperature, start/stop times, and whether chloroform was added in one shot. Historical yield comparisons are meaningless without those.

7

Isolation: filter and wash like the sources specify

After the heating period, cool the mixture, then filter the solid by suction filtration.

Wash pattern (patent / process wording, same family as Palkin isolation):

  • Wash with 95% ethanol (example totals on the order of tens of mL depending on batch size — e.g. ~20 parts alcohol in one patent example).
  • Wash with diethyl ether.
  • Wash with water thoroughly to remove residual sulfides (example: ~50 parts water in that patent example).
  • Because the cake is water-wet after that, wash again with alcohol, then ether.
  • Dry the crystals.

Appearance of the isolated dye: green crystals (often described as dark green with metallic character). The wet reaction mixture itself may look dark blue before the washed solid is judged by eye.

Why the water wash exists: sulfide residues are not something you want left in a sensitizing dye cake.

8

Drying and storage

Dry the washed product protected from strong light. Process literature is explicit: keep the green crystals in a cool, dry place, well protected from light.

Solutions are less stable than the solid. If the dye will be used for screens or spectroscopic checks, dissolve only what is needed and prefer fresh solutions.

Labeling: name (dicyanine / dicyanine A), date, route (Palkin-style sulfide/CHCl3), approximate mass, and hazard marking.

9

How the older Mikeska route differs (so you don’t mix recipes)

Do not blend steps from the two methods unless you are reading the originals side by side on purpose.

Mikeska-style condensation sketch:

  • Often converts ethiodide to ethnitrate with silver nitrate in alcohol, filters off silver iodide, concentrates.
  • Uses sodium methylate or ethylate in absolute alcohol.
  • Air present in the classic description.
  • Yield of dye was small (Palkin’s comparative average ~0.025 g per g intermediate).

Palkin-style condensation sketch:

  • Uses ethiodide directly in many of the sulfide experiments.
  • Alkalinity from alcoholic sodium sulfide.
  • Small controlled chloroform addition.
  • 95% ethanol medium preferred in his study.
  • Much higher dye recovery under his best conditions.
10

Quality checks, failure modes, and hard limits

Positive signs:

  • Deep blue reaction liquor during condensation
  • Filterable solid after cooling
  • Dark green crystalline washed product
  • Intense blue-green/violet color when a speck dissolves in ethanol

Common failure modes in historical practice:

  • Dirty or wet intermediate → tar, low dye
  • Wrong sulfide strength or skipped filtration of sulfide stock → messy solids
  • Chloroform omitted or badly proportioned → yield collapse versus Palkin’s best runs
  • Overheating / excessive time → side dyes and dull product
  • Incomplete sulfide wash → contaminated cake
  • Light exposure during drying/storage → degraded color strength

Hard limit: this sequence belongs in a regulated laboratory with training and waste handling. The detail exists so the historical method is understandable — not so it is casually reproduced.

Sources to read next: Palkin (1923); Mikeska, Haller & Adams (1920); process patents of the same sulfide/chloroform family. See also the site page.

Quick reference card (Palkin-centered)

  • Sulfide stock: 25 g Na2S·9H2O / 100 mL 95% EtOH, boil ~2 min, cool, filter
  • Condensation charge: ~1 g ethiodide / 25 mL sulfide stock
  • Chloroform: ~1.5 mL / g ethiodide, added quickly
  • Heat: controlled bath near ~50°C (trials also explore nearby ranges); patent example often ~2 h at 50°C
  • Workup: cool → suction filter → alcohol → ether → water → alcohol → ether → dry dark
  • Product look: dark green crystals; solutions deep blue-green/violet

Everything that can go wrong — dye synthesis

Not vibes. Concrete failure modes drawn from the chemistry, the 1920/1923 write-ups, and ordinary lab reality.

Before any reaction starts

  • No fume hood / bad airflow: ethyl iodide, chloroform, ether, and sulfide odor all leave the flask. Inhalation exposure is not theoretical.
  • Wrong gloves / wet gloves: organics wick; you get skin exposure and then touch your face, pens, door handles.
  • Food glassware or shared sinks: permanent contamination of items used for eating or handwashing.
  • Unlabeled bottles: next person (or future you) mistakes ethiodide, sulfide stock, or chloroform for something milder.
  • No waste plan: halogenated solvent + sulfide + dye residues mixed into “general trash” or poured down a drain — regulatory and safety failure.
  • Open flame near ether/ethanol: flash fire. Ether vapors travel.
  • Old ether with peroxides: distillation or aggressive evaporation can detonate peroxide concentrates.
  • Missing eye protection: splash of sulfide solution or hot alcohol is a permanent-injury path.

Ethiodide intermediate stage

  • Base and ethyl iodide not actually combined at equal weight: incomplete quaternization; later condensation starts from garbage stoichiometry.
  • Insufficient warming: mixture never fully solidifies; workup is oily sludge instead of a handleable solid.
  • Overheating the quaternization: dark tar, side alkylation, material that will not crystallize cleanly.
  • Skipping the ether wash: residual ethyl iodide stays in the cake (toxicity + stench + later side reactions).
  • One lazy recrystallization from ethanol: colored gums stay in; Palkin-type condensation then yields tar or microscopic dye amounts.
  • Using wet intermediate: water in the solid changes the alcoholic sulfide reaction; yields drop; mixtures look wrong.
  • Thinking the intermediate is already the green dye: process stops early; nothing useful is made.
  • Silver-nitrate ethnitrate detour done carelessly (Mikeska path): incomplete iodide removal, silver salts in filtrate, false confidence in “clear” solution.

Alcoholic sodium sulfide stock

  • Wrong hydrate math: charging anhydrous sulfide as if it were Na2S·9H2O (or the reverse) makes the alkalinity/sulfide strength wrong.
  • Not boiling ~2 minutes: undissolved solids, weak supernatant, unreproducible concentration.
  • Not filtering/decanting: grit carries into condensation; filtration of dye becomes miserable; side surfaces catalyze mess.
  • Using absolute alcohol when the studied process preferred 95%: not automatically fatal, but you are no longer on Palkin’s validated medium.
  • Using methylated spirits / unknown denaturants: denaturants interfere; color and solubility go off-script.
  • Stock sits open absorbing CO2/moisture: titer drifts; today’s “25 g/100 mL” is not yesterday’s.
  • Acid contacts sulfide solution: hydrogen sulfide release — acute toxic gas event.
  • Metal tools that sulfide attacks: contamination and container failure.

Condensation (sulfide + chloroform)

  • Ratio ignored (not ~1 g ethiodide per 25 mL stock): either starved reaction or a soup of side products.
  • Chloroform omitted: in Palkin’s comparisons this collapses yield toward the poor older numbers.
  • Chloroform poured slowly “carefully” when the method says add quickly: local concentration gradients; unreproducible runs.
  • Chloroform grossly overcharged: waste, worse separations, more halogenated waste, not free extra dye.
  • Temperature too low / time too short: weak color, little precipitate, mostly recoverable intermediate contamination.
  • Temperature too high / boil-over: solvent loss changes concentrations mid-reaction; bumping throws sulfide/organics up the flask.
  • No stirring when solids form: crusting, hot spots, incomplete conversion.
  • Open flask in a horizontal airstream: chloroform and ethanol evaporate; stoichiometry silently changes.
  • Scale-up without thermal control: lab notebook times from 1 g runs do not transfer to large flasks without re-mapping heat-up.
  • Mixing Mikeska ethylate steps into a sulfide recipe mid-way: neither method is followed; product is undefined.

Isolation and washing

  • Filtering hot: dye stays dissolved; yield appears to “vanish.”
  • Skipping suction / using gravity only on fine dye: slow oxidation/contact time; losses on paper.
  • Skipping water wash: sulfide residues remain in the cake (odor, corrosion, contaminated “product”).
  • Stopping after water wash without alcohol/ether again: wet cake clumps, dries as dirty bricks, traps impurities.
  • Washing with huge volumes of ethanol indiscriminately: product dissolves away — green filtrate means you washed your yield down the flask.
  • Paper tears / solid runs through: fine dye is easy to lose; clear filtrate is not always a good sign.
  • Contaminating the cake with dirty spatulas or used paper: fibers and grit become part of the “crystalline” solid.

Drying, storage, and later handling

  • Drying under strong light: cyanine dyes fade/decompose; later solutions look weak.
  • Storing warm or in a sunny window: same photothermal degradation.
  • Leaving containers unlabeled: toxic green powder without context is a lab incident waiting.
  • Making large ethanol solutions “for later”: solution-phase dye dies faster than solid; screens and tests then use dead material.
  • Dusting dye into the air when weighing: inhalation/contamination of the balance area.
  • Assuming commercial green powder is dicyanine without provenance: wrong dye → wrong spectra → meaningless screen tests.

Human / procedural failure modes

  • Fatigue rushing washes at night: skipped sulfide removal is the usual casualty.
  • Two people using the same sulfide stock without noting level: concentration no longer matches the notebook.
  • Copying a blog ratio that already mixed Palkin and Mikeska: guaranteed confusion.
  • Treating a failed batch as “close enough” for eye experiments: unknown mixture, unknown toxicity profile.
  • No incident plan for eye splash: seconds matter; improvisation fails.

Screen materials

Kilner-style screens were basically two pieces of glass with a thin layer of dye solution sealed between them — like a very flat, transparent “sandwich.”

Core materials

  • Dicyanin dye (solid) — the coloring agent. Toxic, scarce, light-sensitive. Historical workers used tiny amounts.
  • Ethanol (high purity) — dissolves the dye into a clear, deeply colored liquid for the cell.
  • Two flat optical glass plates — clean, smooth, same size. Window glass can be flat enough for crude demos; optical plate glass is what you want for fewer distortions.
  • Spacers — thin strips that set the liquid thickness. Historical practical range is often described around 0.1–0.5 mm. Too thick = muddy dark; too thin = barely any color.
  • Sealant — must tolerate alcohol (many ordinary craft glues fail). The point is a continuous edge seal so ethanol cannot evaporate and air cannot creep in.
  • Pipette or dropper — to place solution without flooding the edges early.
  • Lint-free cloth / lens tissue + alcohol — for cleaning glass.
  • Dark storage — finished cells and stock solution hate bright light and heat.

Solution strength (historical order of magnitude)

  • A common later retelling uses about 0.1 g dye in 100 mL ethanol (roughly 1:1000 by weight/volume thinking).
  • Exact shade depends on path length (spacer thickness) as much as concentration. Kilner described darker and lighter screens for different stages of viewing.

Optional / practical extras

  • Gloves and eye protection when handling dye powder or strong solutions
  • Marker to label “dark screen” vs “light screen” if you make more than one thickness/concentration
  • A simple stand or frame so you are not holding cold glass against your face for long sessions

Most modern products sold as “dicyanin glasses” are ordinary colored plastic/glass filters — not sealed alcoholic dye cells.

Make the screens

This is a fully detailed historical construction and use guide for Kilner-style dicyanin screens, based on Walter J. Kilner’s The Human Atmosphere (1911) and later practical retellings of the alcoholic glass-cell method. It explains materials, concentrations, assembly, sealing failures, viewing setup, and what is not proven. Authentic dicyanin is toxic and scarce.

Step 1 of 12
1

What a Kilner screen is (and is not)

In its successful form, Kilner’s screen is a liquid optical cell: two glass surfaces with a thin layer of dicyanin dissolved in alcohol sealed between them.

It is not:

  • ordinary blue sunglasses
  • gelatine film that stays stable forever
  • a proven medical scanner
  • proof of a supernatural aura

Kilner himself tested coatings first and abandoned them when they failed chemically. The alcohol-filled glass cell was the form that lasted long enough to use.

2

What Kilner tried before liquid cells (so you don’t repeat dead ends)

From the 1911 book’s own narrative:

  • Glass coated with collodion or gelatine dyed with dicyanin — “entirely useless” because decomposition began immediately.
  • A celluloid solution (Zapon) — better at first, but color was lost within a few hours.
  • Alcohol solutions of different strengths in glass cells — “on the whole satisfactory,” though color can still drift over time even when kept dark.

That is why every serious later description returns to sealed alcoholic solutions between glass, not painted films.

3

How many screens and why two strengths

Kilner states that, as a rule, only two screens are necessary:

  • one alcoholic dicyanin solution of a certain strength
  • a second that is less dilute (stronger / darker)

He also mentions experimental screens with other strengths and other dyes, but those were side trials. The working kit idea is: a darker screen and a lighter screen.

Later popular writers sometimes call the darker one a “sensitising” screen and the lighter one a viewing screen. The exact ritual varies by retelling; the physical fact does not: denser color filters the spectrum more aggressively.

4

Solution chemistry and concentration (be specific)

Solvent: alcohol — historically ethanol. Kilner’s successful cells are alcoholic. Practical later notes stress: do not use water as the main solvent; aqueous dicyanin decomposes rapidly and wastes dye.

Order-of-magnitude stock used in photographic dicyanin literature and later screen retellings:

  • 0.1 g dicyanin (or dicyanin A) in 100 mL alcohol → nominal 1:1000 stock
  • Keep this stock dark; usable for a limited time (often described as weeks at best); prefer fresh

For two screens:

  • Darker cell: use a stronger blue coloration (higher concentration and/or thicker path length)
  • Lighter cell: dilute until the tint is a weaker blue/blue-green when viewed through the finished thickness

There is no single universal “one drop per millilitre” commandment in Kilner that replaces judgment by eye for the two working densities. Path length (spacer thickness) and concentration trade off.

Safety: weigh and dissolve in a controlled space; treat powder as toxic; no foodware.

5

Glass: what to use

Best: two flat, preferably optically worked glass plates of the same size, free of waves and scratches.

Acceptable historical substitute discussed in later hobby literature: a small flat-sided glass bottle filled and tightly stoppered — not elegant, but it creates a liquid path without fabricating a cell. Large bottles waste expensive solution.

Commercial “Aurospecs”-era claims (post-Kilner marketing) described optically perfect glasses cemented with separators enclosing alcoholised dicyanine — the same sandwich idea, sold as a finished article.

6

Cleaning (non-negotiable)

Fingerprints, dust, and dried water marks become permanent optical defects once sealed.

  • Wash both inner faces with alcohol or a residue-poor glass cleaner
  • Wipe with lint-free lens tissue
  • Inspect by reflection under hard light
  • Dry fully before solution touches the glass

Handle plates by edges only after cleaning.

7

Spacers and path length

For a plate cell, set a narrow gap with inert spacers along the border (foil, thin glass strips, or other alcohol-tolerant spacers used in optical cell work).

Practical thickness band often cited for handmade cells: about 0.1–0.5 mm. This is a workshop range, not a Kilner commandment carved in stone — but it matches the need for a thin, even liquid sheet rather than a cuvette-deep tank.

  • Thicker gap → more absorption → darker image, more dye used
  • Thinner gap → lighter color for the same concentration

If building two screens, keep spacer thickness consistent within each cell so the color is even across the field of view.

8

Filling the cell without trapping a disaster

  1. Place the bottom plate flat with spacers positioned.
  2. Pipette solution into the center/edge reservoir until the volume will fill the gap.
  3. Lower the top plate like a hinge — contact one edge first — so air is pushed out.
  4. Apply gentle even pressure so the liquid sheet is continuous.

Bubbles: work small bubbles to the perimeter. Large trapped voids mean reopen, clean if needed, and refill. A sealed bubble is a permanent blind spot.

Overflow: wipe edges clean before sealant; dye solution on the outside becomes a contamination and adhesion problem.

9

Sealing, curing, labeling

Seal the entire perimeter with a cement/sealant that tolerates alcohol. The historical requirement is simple: liquid-tight.

If the seal is imperfect:

  • alcohol evaporates
  • concentration drifts upward, then crusts form
  • air blebs appear later

Later commercial refills existed precisely because evaporation was a known failure mode.

Label: “dark” or “light,” approximate concentration, solvent, date. Store flat, cool, dark.

10

Kilner’s viewing context (as written, not as mythology)

Kilner describes using the screens as optical filters while observing a person against controlled backgrounds and lighting. He preferred arrangements that avoided visual chaos (the book discusses room/light discipline rather than “any bright café”).

He also reported that prolonged use could make the eyes painful — a straightforward warning about staring through dense color filters for a long time.

He claimed training effects and aura morphology. Those claims were not established as scientific fact. Modern reading: strong spectral filtering + afterimages + expectation can produce striking visual impressions without new physics. See .

11

Troubleshooting table

  • Color vanishes in hours (coated film methods): expected for collodion/gelatine/Zapon approaches Kilner already rejected.
  • Cell dries in days: seal leak; ethanol left through a gap.
  • Cloudy contents: moisture, dirty glass, or dye precipitation from aged solution.
  • Uneven color blotches: incomplete fill, residual grease, or spacer steps.
  • Too dark to use: dilute the solution or reduce path length.
  • Almost no tint: solution too weak or gap too thin; verify the dye actually dissolved (dicyanin should give a strong color even at low mass loading).
  • Color “dies” in storage: photochemical/thermal degradation — make fresh stock, keep dark.
12

Ethics, safety, and modern substitutes (clarity)

Safety: authentic dicyanin powder and strong solutions are toxic lab materials. Do not improvise PPE. Do not use food containers.

Scarcity: true dicyanin is not a normal retail craft dye. Many modern “aura goggles” are simply colored glass/plastic.

Substitutes people mention in later literature (cobalt glass, other cyanine dyes, etc.) change the filter spectrum; they are not automatically “the same instrument Kilner used.”

Bottom line: you can understand and document the historical apparatus in extreme detail. That does not convert aura claims into validated medicine or physics.

Quick reference card (screens)

  • Preferred form: alcohol + dicyanin in a sealed glass cell (not collodion films)
  • Stock scale often cited: 0.1 g / 100 mL alcohol (1:1000), kept dark, used fresh
  • Working kit idea: two strengths (darker + lighter)
  • Path length: thin even sheet (workshop band often ~0.1–0.5 mm for plate cells)
  • Failure modes: evaporation, bubbles, dirty glass, dye fade
  • Kilner warning: long sessions can pain the eyes

Everything that can go wrong — screens

Physical cell failures, solution failures, viewing failures, and safety failures — listed as concrete events.

Solution preparation failures

  • Using water as the main solvent: dicyanin decomposes quickly in water; color dies; dye is wasted.
  • Methylated spirit with unknown denaturants: haze, odd colors, sealant attack, unreproducible tint.
  • Undissolved particles thought to be “normal”: grit in the cell becomes permanent speckles; true dissolution was never achieved.
  • Stock far stronger than intended: finished cell is opaque; user cranks brightness and strains eyes harder.
  • Stock far weaker than intended: almost no filtering effect; people conclude “dicyanin does nothing” when the cell is basically clear glass.
  • Old stock used because it “still looks a bit blue”: degraded dye + unknown byproducts; optical behavior is not Kilner’s solution anymore.
  • Weighing error on 0.1 g scale without a proper balance: concentration can easily be 2× or 0.2× intended.
  • Cross-contaminated spatula from sulfide/dye synthesis residues: haze and side colors in the cell.

Glass and cleanliness failures

  • Fingerprints on inner faces: greasy islands; solution dewetts; blotchy color field.
  • Dust sealed in: bright specks under light; looks like “structure” that is only dirt.
  • Dried water spots: mineral rings that never disappear.
  • Warped or prismatic glass: image distortion mistaken for “aura effects.”
  • Scratched glass: permanent lines through every observation.
  • Two plates of different size/thickness: seal geometry fails at edges; leaks start at the mismatch.

Spacer / thickness failures

  • No spacers (plates squeezed dry): almost no liquid path; weak color; or pressure cracks glass.
  • Spacers only on one side: wedge-shaped cell; color gradient across the view.
  • Spacers too thick: very dark field; subject detail vanishes; user stares harder and hurts their eyes.
  • Spacers compressible (soft plastic that swells in alcohol): thickness changes over hours; seal stresses; leaks.
  • Spacers soluble in alcohol: gap collapses; contamination in the liquid.

Filling and bubble failures

  • Dropping the top plate flat from above: large trapped air sheet; permanent void.
  • Not enough liquid: voids at corners that look like “breaks in the field.”
  • Too much liquid before closing: floods edges; dye solution under future sealant path; seal never bonds.
  • Chasing microbubbles for an hour with dirty tools: re-contaminates clean glass.
  • Filling outdoors in wind: dust nucleation sites everywhere.

Sealing and aging failures

  • Craft glue attacked by ethanol: seal softens, clouds, or dissolves; cell empties into a sticky mess.
  • Incomplete perimeter seal: slow evaporation over days; concentration creeps up; then dry crust.
  • Pinholes at corners: classic leak points; bubbles grow inward from the corner.
  • Handling before cure: seal bead shifts; microchannels form.
  • Thermal cycling (hot car, cold room): expansion pulls seal off glass; sudden bubble appearance.
  • Storing on edge with weak seal: hydrostatic pressure finds the weak point.
  • Refilling without full clean: old oxidized dye seeds cloudiness in new solution.

Coating methods Kilner already found fail

  • Collodion-dyed coats: decompose immediately — Kilner called them entirely useless.
  • Gelatine-dyed coats: same class of failure; not a stable screen.
  • Zapon / celluloid-type films: may look good briefly, then lose color within hours.
  • Reinventing those coatings anyway: guarantees a short-lived object and wasted dye.

Viewing and interpretation failures

  • Staring too long through a dark filter: eye pain (Kilner warned); temporary afterimages mistaken for “fields.”
  • Uncontrolled lighting / busy background: contrast artifacts everywhere; pure noise.
  • Expecting a medical diagnosis: no validated diagnostic protocol exists from this apparatus.
  • Using any blue plastic sunglasses and calling them dicyanin screens: spectrum and path length are not the historical cell.
  • Confirmation bias with a second observer coached on what to see: reports align socially, not optically.
  • Dehydration, fatigue, migraine aura, or pressure phosphenes: real visual phenomena misattributed to the screen.

Safety failures specific to screens

  • Dye solution on skin while “just adjusting the plate”: toxic exposure without noticing.
  • Broken cell in a bag or drawer: alcohol + dye soaks into fabric; later skin contact.
  • Children handling pretty blue glass: breakage + ingestion risk.
  • Inhaling powder while topping up a cell: weighing without containment.
  • Disposing of broken cells in household trash without containment: other people get cut and exposed.

Documentation failures (why “it didn’t work” becomes meaningless)

  • No record of concentration, solvent grade, path length, or date
  • No note of whether the cell leaked overnight
  • Swapping dark/light screens without labeling
  • Comparing results from a degraded month-old fill to a fresh one as if identical

Questions

Still manufactured?

Not in any ordinary commercial sense.

Real aura?

No solid scientific evidence. Filters + expectation suffice.

Why photography?

Early plates were weak in the red; dyes extended the spectrum.

Modern “dicyanin glasses”?

Safe at home?

Synthesis: no. Casual handling of authentic dye: no.

Side-by-side of the two laboratory lineages most often confused online.

Alkaline agentSodium methylate / ethylate in absolute alcoholSodium sulfide in 95% alcohol

IntermediateOften via ethnitrate (AgNO3)Often ethiodide directly in sulfide trials
