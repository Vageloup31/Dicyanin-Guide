Overview

What is dicyanin?

Dicyanin (dicyanine / dicyanine A) is a synthetic carbocyanine dye — two nitrogen-containing rings joined by a short conjugated carbon chain. A common form is the iodide salt, roughly C25H25IN2.

It was developed in the early 20th century as a photographic sensitizer. Ordinary silver-halide plates mostly “saw” blue light; dyes like dicyanin pushed useful sensitivity into the red and toward the near-infrared.

Appearance

Dark green crystals with metallic sheen. Solutions: intense blue-green to violet.

Original use

Extending plate response toward deep red / near-IR.

Kilner link

Alcoholic solutions sealed between glass for 1911 screen experiments.

Today

Obscure, toxic, unstable — not a normal commercial product.

Critical

Safety

Not a home project

Dicyanin is toxic. Historical synthesis uses ethyl iodide, sodium sulfide, chloroform, and strong bases — flammable, reactive, or regulated chemicals.

Work only in a proper laboratory with a fume hood, training, and full PPE. This site is historical documentation.

Kilner’s aura claims were never independently scientifically validated.

Hazards from the old chemistry

  • Toxic dye dust and residues
  • Flammable solvents (ethanol, ether)
  • Chloroform vapor
  • Sulfide solutions (odor + reactivity)
  • Eye strain from prolonged viewing through dense filters
Chemistry

Properties

Dicyanin belongs to the cyanine / carbocyanine family. The conjugated bridge between the rings creates strong visible absorption — the reason these dyes worked as sensitizers.

  • Solid: dark green, often with metallic luster
  • Solution: deep blue-green to violet; can show dichroism
  • Absorption: deep red region (historically discussed near ~670 nm in some preparations)
  • Stability: limited — light, heat, and time degrade solutions
  • Classic screen solvent: ethanol
Materials

Dye materials

Chemicals (historical lab scale)

  • 2,4-Dimethyl-6-ethoxyquinoline
  • Ethyl iodide (iodoethane)
  • Sodium sulfide nonahydrate
  • 95% ethanol and absolute ethanol
  • Chloroform (catalytic, Palkin method)
  • Diethyl ether (washing)

Equipment

  • Standard organic glassware
  • Controlled heating bath ~50°C / steam bath
  • Suction filtration and recrystallization setup
  • Fume hood, chemical-resistant gloves, goggles, lab coat
Step-by-step

Make the dye

Outline of the Palkin-style laboratory sequence. Historical procedure only — not permission for home chemistry.

1

Prepare the ethiodide intermediate

Combine 2,4-dimethyl-6-ethoxyquinoline with roughly an equal weight of ethyl iodide. Warm briefly until solid. Wash with diethyl ether. Recrystallize several times from boiling 95% ethanol. Dry.

2

Make alcoholic sodium sulfide

Boil about 25 g Na2S·9H2O in 100 mL of 95% ethanol for ~2 minutes. Cool and filter.

3

Condense to form the dye

Use about 1 g ethiodide per 25 mL sulfide solution. Dissolve intermediate, add about 1.5 mL chloroform per gram as catalyst, hold near 50°C for about 30 minutes. Cool — the dye precipitates.

4

Isolate and wash

Filter by suction. Wash with 95% ethanol, then diethyl ether, then water; wash again with ethanol and ether. Air-dry protected from strong light. Product: dark green crystalline solid with metallic sheen.

Materials

Screen materials

For a Kilner-style cell

  • Dicyanin (toxic, scarce, light-sensitive)
  • High-purity ethanol
  • Two clean flat optical glass plates
  • Thin spacers (~0.1–0.5 mm)
  • Alcohol-compatible sealant
  • Pipette, clean vial, dark cool storage, basic PPE

True dicyanin is hard to source. Many modern “aura glasses” are ordinary colored filters, not sealed dye cells.

Step-by-step

Make the screens

Historical pattern for a Kilner-style liquid cell. Documentation of an old method — not a recommendation to handle toxic dye casually.

1

Make a dilute stock solution

On the order of 0.1 g in 100 mL ethanol (~1:1000) in later retellings. Deep blue-green to violet. Keep cool, dark, reasonably fresh.

2

Clean the glass

Remove all dust, grease, and fingerprints from both plates. Dry completely.

3

Build the liquid gap

Place thin spacers along the edges (~0.1–0.5 mm). Pipette dye solution to fill the gap. Lower the second plate carefully; work out air bubbles.

4

Seal the edges

Seal all around so ethanol cannot evaporate. Result: a flat glass sandwich with a thin colored liquid layer inside.

5

Historical viewing pattern

Controlled light (Kilner preferred a north window and dark background). Brief view of bright sky through a darker screen, then observe a subject through a lighter screen. He warned that long sessions could cause eye pain.

Timeline

History

Early 1900s

Cyanine sensitizing dyes, including dicyanin-type materials, enter photographic chemistry.

1911

Walter J. Kilner publishes The Human Atmosphere, describing dicyanin screens.

1912

Kilner’s note on chromatic focus shifts after using a dicyanin screen.

1920

Mikeska, Haller & Adams publish a synthesis of dicyanine A in JACS.

1923

Palkin reports improved laboratory conditions and better recovery.

1924+

Structural work clarifies the carbocyanine nature of the dicyanines. Photography moves toward more stable sensitizers.

FAQ

Common questions

Is dicyanin still manufactured?

Not in any ordinary commercial sense. It is obscure, toxic, and unstable.

Did the screens show a real aura?

There is no good scientific evidence for that. Ordinary effects from strong colored filters — afterimages, contrast changes, expectation — explain the reports more simply.

Why did photographers want this dye?

Early plates ignored much of the red end of the spectrum. Sensitizing dyes extended the usable range toward deep red and near-IR.

Are modern “dicyanin glasses” authentic?

Usually not. Most are standard colored filters or marketing approximations, not sealed historical dye cells.

Can this be done safely at home?

Synthesis: no. Casual handling of authentic dye: no. This page documents historical practice only.
